## Pacing and execution

Build a race plan from the target duration, terrain, environment, recent evidence, and current recovery rather than a single predicted finish time. Start conservatively enough to preserve execution late in the event. If the athlete's recent training or recovery contradicts the original target, state the trade-off and recommend an adjusted pacing or completion objective.

Source: [Bourdon et al., training-load monitoring consensus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28463642/).

## Fueling, hydration, and heat

Practice nutrition and hydration strategies during representative training. Heat, altitude, travel, illness, gastrointestinal symptoms, medications, and individual sweat losses can materially change a plan. Do not prescribe fluid, sodium, supplement, or weight-loss protocols as medical advice; recommend a sports dietitian or clinician when risk factors are present.

Source: [Nutrition and Athletic Performance position statement](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11145214/); [ISSN ultra-marathon position stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31699159/).

## Time, stress, and schedule constraints

When time is limited, preserve the few sessions most relevant to the athlete's current goal and make the rest easy or optional. Sleep disruption, work stress, travel, and family commitments are training-load inputs, not moral failures. Replace missed volume cautiously instead of compressing it into a single catch-up session.

Source: [Kellmann et al., Recovery and Performance in Sport consensus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29345524/).
