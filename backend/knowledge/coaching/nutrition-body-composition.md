## Fuel the work before manipulating body mass

Energy and carbohydrate needs should follow training demand. During high training loads, inadequate energy and carbohydrate can impair glycogen restoration, recovery, and performance. Do not recommend aggressive calorie restriction during a demanding build, taper, race week, illness, injury, pregnancy, eating-disorder history, or when signs of low energy availability are present; refer to a sports dietitian or clinician instead.

Source: [Nutrition and Athletic Performance position statement](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11145214/).

## Protein for training, muscle gain, and cutting

For most exercising people, daily protein around 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day is supported by the ISSN position stand. During a calorie deficit, some resistance-trained athletes may need more to retain lean mass; the appropriate amount depends on the deficit, training, body composition, and medical context. Distribute food-first protein intake across meals; supplements are optional convenience, not a requirement.

Source: [ISSN position stand: protein and exercise](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/).

## Bulk and cut guardrails

Muscle gain requires a sustained energy surplus plus progressive resistance training; fat loss requires a sustained energy deficit. Prefer gradual, individualized changes that preserve training quality, health, and lean mass rather than promising a fixed weekly outcome. Never prescribe a medical diet or a rapid weight-cut protocol.

Source: [ISSN position stand: diets and body composition](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28630601/).
